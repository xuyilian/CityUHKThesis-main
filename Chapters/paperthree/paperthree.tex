\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

All flights were conducted indoors over a still water surface, within a
motion-capture volume that provided the position and attitude feedback for
the controller.

\paragraph{Motion capture.}
Vehicle pose is measured by an OptiTrack system running Motive, which
reconstructs rigid-body position and orientation from clusters of retro-%
reflective markers and streams them to the ground station over UDP.  Two
rigid bodies are tracked in every flight: the vehicle itself, and the water
container, so that the target for the descent is measured rather than assumed
and the vehicle can be commanded to a point above the water without either
being surveyed in advance.
% TODO(setup): camera count, tracked volume dimensions, and mocap frame rate.

\paragraph{Water.}
Hops are performed over a container of still water, tracked as the second
rigid body above.
% TODO(setup): container dimensions and water depth. Depth matters only in
% that it must exceed the foil penetration; state it so the reader can rule
% out a bottom effect.
The surface is left undisturbed before each attempt, so that the entry
conditions match the flat-surface assumption of the model in
Chapter~\ref{dynamic}.  No attempt was made to control or characterise the
surface state after a hop, which becomes relevant when hops are repeated in
quick succession (Section~\ref{sec:exp_results}).

\paragraph{Vehicle.}
The vehicle is the $90\;\mathrm{g}$ airframe described in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperone}: a Crazyflie Bolt flight controller, four
Sparis \mbox{1204} motors of which two are mounted vertically for lift and
two tangentially to drive the spin, and four hydrofoils spanning
$75$--$145\;\mathrm{mm}$ from the spin axis at a fixed inclination of
$30^{\circ}$.  Marker clusters are mounted on the airframe such that they do
not shadow one another as the vehicle rotates.

\paragraph{Control and communication.}
The controller of Chapter~\ref{chapter:papertwo} runs off-board on a ground
station at $100\;\mathrm{Hz}$, taking mocap pose as its only exteroceptive
input.  Commands reach the vehicle over a $2.4\;\mathrm{GHz}$ radio link as a
compressed nine-byte packet carrying the two horizontal command components,
the measured yaw angle, and the collective thrust.  Sending the yaw angle
rather than a body-frame attitude command allows the projection
of~\eqref{eq:torque_projection} to be evaluated on board at the attitude-loop
rate, so that the command remains correctly phased with the vehicle's
rotation without requiring the outer loop to run at the spin frequency.  The
tangential rotors are held at a fixed thrust setpoint configured once at
connection and are not modulated in flight.

\paragraph{Logging.}
Vehicle state is logged on the ground station at the control rate: mocap
position and attitude, the filtered attitude estimates of
Section~\ref{sec:nlms}, the commands issued, and the measured yaw rate.
Battery voltage and the onboard vertical accelerometer are streamed from the
vehicle at $100\;\mathrm{Hz}$ over the same link.  All quantities reported in
this chapter are drawn from these logs, with the exception of the
qualitative descriptions of vehicle behaviour, which are taken from
synchronised video.

\section{Online Separation of Rotation-Induced State Estimation Oscillations}
\label{sec:nlms}

The vehicle's pose is measured by a motion capture (mocap) system, which
reconstructs the rigid-body position and orientation quaternion from a cluster
of reflective markers attached to the airframe.  The relevant attitude states
for the position controller are the rotation-matrix entries $R_{13}$ and
$R_{23}$, which project the body $z$-axis onto the world $x$- and $y$-axes
and encode the vehicle's lateral tilt.

Because the vehicle operates in a sustained high-yaw-rate relaxed-hover
condition, a systematic periodic artifact appears in the mocap-derived attitude.
The mocap system locates the vehicle's body-frame origin at the geometric
centroid of the marker cluster; this centroid does not coincide with the true
center of mass, which is the actual pivot of the yaw rotation.  As the vehicle
spins, the offset vector from the true COM to the marker centroid sweeps through
a full revolution in the horizontal plane at the instantaneous yaw frequency
$f_0$.  From the inertial frame, this manifests as a sinusoidal oscillation in
$R_{13}$ and $R_{23}$ at frequency $f_0$, superimposed on the true
quasi-static tilt components.

\paragraph{Signal model.}
The measured attitude signals are modeled as
\begin{align}
    R_{13}(t) &= A_{13} + B_{13}\sin\phi(t) + C_{13}\cos\phi(t),
    \label{eq:R13model}\\
    R_{23}(t) &= A_{23} + B_{23}\sin\phi(t) + C_{23}\cos\phi(t),
    \label{eq:R23model}
\end{align}
where $\phi(t)$ is the accumulated yaw phase and $A_{13}$, $A_{23}$ are the
low-frequency components representing the true attitude to be recovered.

\paragraph{Phase tracking.}
The instantaneous yaw rate $\dot\psi$ is obtained by differentiating the mocap
yaw angle after unwrapping to eliminate $\pm180^{\circ}$ discontinuities.  A
first-order exponential moving average (EMA) with coefficient $\alpha_\omega =
0.1$ smooths the rate estimate,
\begin{equation}
    \dot\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{filt}}
        = \dot\psi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{filt}}
        + \alpha_\omega\!\left(\dot\psi_k - \dot\psi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{filt}}\right).
    \label{eq:ema_yawrate}
\end{equation}
The instantaneous frequency and phase are then updated each sample as
\begin{equation}
    f_0(k) = \frac{\dot\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{filt}}}{360^{\circ}},
    \qquad
    \phi(k) = \phi(k-1) + 2\pi f_0(k)\,\Delta t,
    \label{eq:phase_int}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t$ is the sample interval.

\paragraph{Online normalized LMS estimation.}
At each time step $k$ the regressor vector is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}(k)
        = \bigl[1,\;\sin\phi(k),\;\cos\phi(k)\bigr]^{\top}.
\end{equation}
For each channel $i\in\{13,23\}$, the parameter vector
$\boldsymbol{\theta}_i(k) = [A_i,\,B_i,\,C_i]_k^{\top}$ is updated by the
normalized LMS rule
\begin{align}
    e_i(k) &= R_i(k) - \boldsymbol{\theta}_i(k)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varphi}(k),
    \label{eq:nlms_err}\\[2pt]
    \boldsymbol{\theta}_i(k+1)
        &= \boldsymbol{\theta}_i(k)
           + \frac{\mu\,e_i(k)\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}(k)}
                  {\boldsymbol{\varphi}(k)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varphi}(k)+\varepsilon},
    \label{eq:nlms_update}
\end{align}
with step size $\mu = 0.3$ and regularization constant $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$.
The algorithm is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:online_att}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Online NLMS attitude correction}
\label{alg:online_att}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Initialize:} $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{13}\leftarrow\mathbf{0}$,
       $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{23}\leftarrow\mathbf{0}$,
       $\phi\leftarrow 0$,
       $\dot\psi^{\mathrm{filt}}\leftarrow 0$
\For{each sample $k$}
    \State Unwrap mocap yaw; compute raw rate $\dot\psi_k$
    \State $\dot\psi_k^{\mathrm{filt}} \leftarrow
            \dot\psi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{filt}}
            + \alpha_\omega(\dot\psi_k - \dot\psi_{k-1}^{\mathrm{filt}})$
    \State $\phi(k) \leftarrow \phi(k-1)
            + 2\pi\,\bigl(\dot\psi_k^{\mathrm{filt}}/360^{\circ}\bigr)\Delta t$
    \State $\boldsymbol{\varphi} \leftarrow
            [1,\,\sin\phi(k),\,\cos\phi(k)]^{\top}$
    \For{$i \in \{13,23\}$}
        \State $e_i \leftarrow R_i(k) - \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varphi}$
        \State $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_i
                + \mu\,e_i\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}
                  \;/\;(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\varepsilon)$
    \EndFor
    \State Extract corrected attitude:
           $\hat A_{13}(k) \leftarrow \theta_{13,1}$,\;
           $\hat A_{23}(k) \leftarrow \theta_{23,1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Once the estimate has converged, the first coefficient $A_i(k)$ gives the
running estimate of the true, low-frequency tilt.  An optional second-stage EMA
with coefficient $\alpha_A = 0.3$ further smooths this estimate before it is
forwarded to the position controller,
\begin{equation}
    \hat A_i(k)
        = \hat A_i(k-1)
          + \alpha_A\bigl(A_i(k) - \hat A_i(k-1)\bigr).
    \label{eq:ema_A}
\end{equation}
The filtered estimates $\hat A_{13}$ and $\hat A_{23}$ replace the raw $R_{13}$
and $R_{23}$ in the attitude feedback loop, effectively removing the
spin-frequency artifact from the state estimate seen by the controller.

\section{Constant-Height Jumping on Water}
\label{sec:constant_height_jumping}

A single hop demonstrates the mechanism; a sequence of them demonstrates that
the mechanism can be sustained.  This section describes how the vehicle is
driven through repeated hops, each returning it to the same commanded height,
so that successive contacts occur under nominally identical conditions.

\paragraph{The hop cycle.}
One cycle proceeds through four stages.  The vehicle first holds position at a
commanded height above the water under the controller of
Chapter~\ref{chapter:papertwo}.  The lift command is then removed (both lift rotors are commanded to zero thrust) and the vehicle descends unpowered onto
the surface.  Throughout this descent the tangential rotors remain at their
fixed setpoint, so the hydrofoils arrive at the water still sweeping at close
to their hover speed; this is the property that makes an unpowered descent
viable at all, and it is a direct consequence of decoupling the yaw drive from
the lift channel (Section~\ref{sec:attitude_dynamics}).  The foils then
submerge, reverse the vehicle's vertical velocity, and eject it.  Finally the
controller is re-engaged and returns the vehicle to the commanded height,
whereupon the cycle repeats.

\paragraph{Why the descent is left unpowered.}
Cutting the lift entirely is a stronger test than assisting the ejection, and
it isolates the contribution of the hydrofoils: whatever upward velocity the
vehicle leaves the water with has been supplied by the water, not by the
rotors.  A facility was provided in the flight software to apply thrust during
the descent, but it was set to zero for every flight reported here and proved
unnecessary.

\paragraph{Detecting the hop.}
Re-engaging the controller at the right instant is the practical difficulty in
closing the cycle.  Too early and the controller fights the contact; too late
and the vehicle has already fallen back.  Rather than time the transition
open-loop, it is triggered from the measured motion.  The vehicle is armed
once a genuine descent is detected (the filtered vertical velocity falling
below a threshold), and the transition fires when that velocity reverses and
the vehicle is rising while still close to the surface.  Both conditions must
be met, so that neither a slow drift nor a spurious velocity excursion can
trigger a handover.

\paragraph{Rejecting tracking artefacts.}
The splash raised on contact is the principal disturbance to the measurement,
not to the vehicle: water thrown across the markers can interrupt tracking at
precisely the instant the transition logic is watching for.  Two failure
signatures were observed and are rejected explicitly.  In the first, the
reported position jumps to an implausible value and the derived velocity
spikes with it; in the second, the reported position freezes at its last valid
value for many consecutive samples while the derived velocity decays smoothly
toward zero, which no genuine motion produces.  Position and velocity
plausibility bounds reject the former, and a count of consecutive identical
position samples rejects the latter.

\paragraph{Recovering from a lost measurement.}
If tracking is lost after the descent has been detected but before the
rebound, the transition cannot fire and the vehicle would otherwise remain in
an unpowered state.  A recovery is therefore armed: a fixed additional thrust
is applied open-loop for a short interval, sufficient to arrest the fall
without steering, after which control is returned to the height controller.
The additional thrust is tapered to zero over the final part of that interval
rather than being removed abruptly, since a step change in thrust at handover
leaves the height controller with a transient it cannot absorb cleanly.  This
recovery is a mitigation for the instrumentation, not part of the locomotion:
it exists so that a tracking dropout costs one hop rather than the flight.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Anatomy of a single hop}

Figure~\ref{fig:hop_single} shows one hop in isolation.  From a hover at
$0.94\;\mathrm{m}$ the lift rotors are commanded to zero thrust and the
vehicle falls freely to the water surface, reaching
$-1.55\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ at entry.  The foils remain in the water for
$106\;\mathrm{ms}$, during which the vehicle descends a further
$86\;\mathrm{mm}$, reverses, and leaves the surface climbing at
$+0.92\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.  The peak upward acceleration during the
contact is $4.9\,g$.  Throughout the fall, the contact, and the ejection the
lift rotors carry no command: the reversal is produced by the hydrofoils
alone.
