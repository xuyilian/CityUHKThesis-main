\chapter{Dynamic Modeling of Water-Skipping Motion}

\label{dynamic}

\section{Overview of Water-Skipping Process}

This chapter models the water-contact phase of a hop.  The vehicle reaches the
surface descending and spinning; during contact the submerged hydrofoils
generate a vertical force, which reverses the descent, and a resisting torque,
which reduces the spin.  The quantities to be predicted are the vertical
velocity and the spin rate at the instant the foils leave the water.

The configuration is that of Figure~\ref{fig:model_definitions}.  Four
hydrofoils are fixed to the airframe and rotate with it about the vertical
axis at yaw rate $\omega$.  A foil occupies the radial band between the root
radius $r_{\mathrm{in}}$, set by the motor-mount circle, and the tip radius
$r_{\mathrm{tip}}$, so an element at radius $r$ sweeps the water at tangential
speed $\omega r$.  Each foil is a flat plate of chord $c$ held at a fixed
inclination $\beta$ to the horizontal.  The vertical position of the vehicle
is $z$, measured from the undisturbed surface and negative below it, and its
vertical velocity is $\dot{z}$.

At the spin rates of interest $\omega r$ exceeds $|\dot{z}|$ by an order of
magnitude, so the resultant flow at an element is nearly horizontal and the
angle of attack is dominated by $\beta$ rather than by the descent.  The force
a foil generates is therefore set by the rotation rather than by the fall.
Because water is roughly three orders of magnitude denser than air, the same
foil sweeping at the same speed generates a force in water that is larger by a
comparable factor, which is why surfaces that are negligible in flight are
sufficient to arrest the descent on contact.

The two effects oppose one another.  The vertical force decelerates and then
reverses the vehicle, while the resisting torque removes the spin that
generates that force, so the force decays over the course of the contact.  A
hop succeeds if the vertical impulse returns the vehicle to the air with enough
spin remaining for it to be controllable.  Sections~\ref{sec:quasi_steady}
and~\ref{sec:vertical_rotational} develop the element forces and the coupled
vertical and rotational motion respectively, and
Section~\ref{sec:simulation} integrates them through a contact.

For reference, the contacts measured in Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperthree} last
between $74$ and $222\;\mathrm{ms}$.  The model developed here predicts a
considerably shorter contact, and that discrepancy is quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:simulation}.

\paragraph{Scope and assumptions.}
Four simplifications are made.  Each is stated here with the fuller treatment it
replaces, so that the cost of making it is visible.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Forces are quasi-steady.}  Each instant is evaluated as if the
    flow were in equilibrium at the local velocity and angle of attack.  An
    unsteady treatment would add an added-mass term proportional to the
    acceleration of the foil and a history term depending on the wake it has
    already shed, both of which are first-order during the entry
    transient~\cite{truscott2014water}.
  \item \emph{The foils are flat plates in separated flow.}  The sectional
    coefficients are those of a thin plate at large angle of attack.  Resolving
    the real section would require the Reynolds-dependent separation and
    reattachment behaviour of the actual profile.
  \item \emph{Only the submerged portion generates force.}  Air loads during
    the contact are neglected.  A coupled treatment would carry both phases and
    the air entrained into the cavity behind the foil.
  \item \emph{The free surface is flat and undisturbed.}  The wetted extent of
    a foil follows from its geometric depth alone.  The full problem tracks the
    surface as it deforms, which determines the cavity that forms behind the
    foil, whether that cavity ventilates to the atmosphere, and how much of the
    foil is in contact with water at all~\cite{truscott2014water}.
\end{enumerate}

The first three are reasonable at this scale.  The fourth is not, and
Section~\ref{sec:model_validation} shows it to be the assumption that limits
the model: the predicted contact is far shorter and harder than the measured
one, in the manner a deforming surface would explain.

\section{Quasi-Steady Blade-Element Model}
\label{sec:quasi_steady}

Because the density of water exceeds that of air by three orders of magnitude,
the force generated by the submerged portion of a hydrofoil dominates the
motion of the vehicle during contact, and only that force is retained here.

The foil is not a single lifting surface with one velocity.  It rotates with
the airframe, so its local speed varies along the span, and it is therefore
treated by the blade-element method: the foil is divided into elements of
radial width $dr$, each evaluated as a two-dimensional section under
quasi-steady conditions, and the element forces are integrated along the span.
The quasi-steady hydrodynamics are introduced directly at the element level
below, since that is the only level at which they are applied.

The chord $c$ denotes the physical streamwise extent of the foil; only its submerged portion $c_{\mathrm{sub}}$ enters the model.}

\paragraph{Element kinematics.}
Figure~\ref{fig:model_definitions}(B) shows the foil in plan view.  It occupies
an annular band of the swept disk, beginning at the root radius
$r_{\mathrm{in}}$ fixed by the motor-mount circle and extending to the tip
radius $r_{\mathrm{tip}}$; its radial span is $r_{\mathrm{tip}}-r_{\mathrm{in}}$,
and no lifting surface occupies $r<r_{\mathrm{in}}$.

An element at radius $r$ meets the water with two velocity components: a
horizontal component due to the rotation of the vehicle, and a vertical
component due to its descent.  The horizontal component is the local
tangential speed
%
\begin{equation}
    v_x(r) = \omega r ,
    \label{eq:blade_tangential_velocity}
\end{equation}
%
which varies along the span, so every quantity derived from it is a function of
$r$.  The vertical component is the descent rate $\dot{z}$, common to the whole
vehicle.  The resultant speed of the element relative to the water is therefore
%
\begin{equation}
    v(r) = \sqrt{(\omega r)^2 + \dot{z}^{\,2}} .
    \label{eq:blade_resultant_velocity}
\end{equation}
%
The angle between this resultant and the horizontal is the inflow angle
%
\begin{equation}
    \gamma(r) = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-\dot{z}}{\omega r}\right),
    \label{eq:blade_gamma}
\end{equation}
%
where $\dot{z}<0$ during the descent, so $-\dot{z}$ is the downward speed.  The
foil is mounted at a fixed inclination $\beta$ to the horizontal, so the local
angle of attack combines that inclination with the inflow direction
(Figure~\ref{fig:model_definitions}(A)):
%
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(r) = \beta + \gamma(r) .
    \label{eq:blade_alpha}
\end{equation}
%
Here $\omega$ is the angular velocity of the vehicle and $\dot{z}$ its vertical
velocity.  Since $\gamma$ depends on $r$, so does $\alpha$: the angle of attack
is largest at the root, where the tangential speed is lowest and the inflow
steepest, and approaches $\beta$ at the tip.

\paragraph{Element forces.}
Under the quasi-steady assumption each element is evaluated as if the flow
around it were in equilibrium, so the standard sectional expressions apply with
the local speed and angle of attack of \eqref{eq:blade_resultant_velocity}
and~\eqref{eq:blade_alpha}.  For a flat plate in separated flow the sectional
coefficients are
%
\begin{equation}
    C_L(\alpha) = 2 \sin{\alpha}\cos{\alpha},
    \qquad
    C_D(\alpha) = 2 \sin^2{\alpha},
    \label{eq:flat_plate_coeffs}
\end{equation}
%
which hold for a thin plate at large angle of attack, where the flow separates
at both edges and the resultant force acts normal to the
plate~\cite{bocquet2003physics,truscott2014water}.

Because the hydrofoil is a thin slanted plate, the portion submerged at penetration depth $z$ extends a length $z/\sin{\beta}$ along the chordwise (slant) direction. This wetted length grows with depth only until the plate is fully wetted, which occurs at the depth $h^{*} = c\sin{\beta}$, where $c$ is the physical chord of the foil; at greater depths the wetted length is bounded by the chord itself. The submerged chord is therefore

\begin{equation}
    c_{\mathrm{sub}} = \min\!\left(\frac{z}{\sin{\beta}},\; c\right),
    \label{eq:submerged_chord}
\end{equation}
and is the same for every blade element at a given instant. The submerged area of a blade element of radial length $dr$ is then

\begin{equation}
    dS = c_{\mathrm{sub}}\,dr .
    \label{eq:blade_area}
\end{equation}

Based on the quasi-steady assumption, the differential lift and drag generated by this blade element are

\begin{equation}
    dF_L = \frac{1}{2}\rho C_L(\alpha) v^2(r)\,c_{\mathrm{sub}}\,dr ,
    \label{eq:differential_lift}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    dF_D = \frac{1}{2}\rho C_D(\alpha) v^2(r)\,c_{\mathrm{sub}}\,dr ,
    \label{eq:differential_drag}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the density of water, and $C_L(\alpha)$ and $C_D(\alpha)$ are the lift and drag coefficients.

The element lift acts perpendicular to the resultant velocity $v$ and the drag
along it, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model_definitions}(A).  Resolving the
pair into vertical and horizontal components is therefore a rotation through
the inflow angle $\gamma$, giving the differential vertical thrust and the
differential horizontal resistance:

\begin{equation}
    dT = dF_L \cos{\gamma(r)} + dF_D \sin{\gamma(r)} ,
    \label{eq:differential_thrust}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    dF_R = dF_D \cos{\gamma(r)} - dF_L \sin{\gamma(r)} .
    \label{eq:differential_resistance}
\end{equation}

The horizontal resistance force produces a resisting torque about the rotational center:

\begin{equation}
    dQ = r\,dF_R
    =
    r\left(dF_D \cos{\gamma(r)} - dF_L \sin{\gamma(r)}\right).
    \label{eq:differential_torque}
\end{equation}


\section{Vertical Motion and Rotational Dynamics}
\label{sec:vertical_rotational}

The total vertical thrust and resisting torque generated by one hydrofoil are obtained by integrating the blade elements along the radial direction, over the span the foil actually occupies: from the root radius $r_{\mathrm{in}}$ to the tip radius $r_{\mathrm{tip}}$. The lower limit is consequential. Because the element force grows as $(\omega r)^2$, extending the integral inward to the axis would not add a small correction but would credit the vehicle with a stretch of foil it does not carry. The chord $c$ enters the model only as the saturation limit of the submerged chord $c_{\mathrm{sub}}$ of~\eqref{eq:submerged_chord}: in the shallow phase of the contact the wetted length is set by the penetration depth alone, while in the deeper phase it is bounded by the physical extent of the foil.

Substituting the differential lift and drag into the force decomposition, the total vertical thrust generated by one hydrofoil is

\begin{equation}
    T_{\mathrm{foil}}
    =
    \int_{r_{\mathrm{in}}}^{r_{\mathrm{tip}}}
    \frac{1}{2}\rho c_{\mathrm{sub}} v^2(r)
    \left[
    C_L(\alpha(r))\cos{\gamma(r)}
    +
    C_D(\alpha(r))\sin{\gamma(r)}
    \right] dr .
    \label{eq:total_thrust_c}
\end{equation}

Similarly, the resisting torque generated by one hydrofoil is

\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{foil}}
    =
    \int_{r_{\mathrm{in}}}^{r_{\mathrm{tip}}}
    \frac{1}{2}\rho c_{\mathrm{sub}} r v^2(r)
    \left[
    C_D(\alpha(r))\cos{\gamma(r)}
    -
    C_L(\alpha(r))\sin{\gamma(r)}
    \right] dr .
    \label{eq:total_torque_c}
\end{equation}

If the vehicle has $N$ identical hydrofoils that contact the water under the same condition, the total vertical thrust and resisting torque are approximated as

\begin{equation}
    T = N T_{\mathrm{foil}},
    \label{eq:total_vehicle_thrust}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    Q = N Q_{\mathrm{foil}}.
    \label{eq:total_vehicle_torque}
\end{equation}

In practice, $N$ represents the number of effective hydrofoils in contact with the water at a given time step.

Combining the above equations, the total vertical thrust and resisting torque can be summarized as nonlinear functions of the vehicle states and hydrofoil design parameters:

\begin{align}
    T &= T\left(
        z,\dot{z},\omega,r_{\mathrm{in}},r_{\mathrm{tip}},c,\beta,N,\rho
    \right),
    \label{eq:T_function} \\[2pt]
    Q &= Q\left(
        z,\dot{z},\omega,r_{\mathrm{in}},r_{\mathrm{tip}},c,\beta,N,\rho
    \right).
    \label{eq:Q_function}
\end{align}

The vertical and rotational dynamics of the vehicle during water impact are then written as

\begin{align}
    m\ddot{z} &= \phantom{-}T\left(
        z,\dot{z},\omega,r_{\mathrm{in}},r_{\mathrm{tip}},c,\beta,N,\rho
    \right) - mg,
    \label{eq:vertical_dynamics_function} \\[2pt]
    I\dot{\omega} &= -Q\left(
        z,\dot{z},\omega,r_{\mathrm{in}},r_{\mathrm{tip}},c,\beta,N,\rho
    \right).
    \label{eq:rotational_dynamics_function}
\end{align}

In this formulation the hydrofoil geometry enters through two parameters: the root and tip radii $r_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{tip}}$, which set the radial integration range $r_{\mathrm{in}} \le r \le r_{\mathrm{tip}}$ ($r_{\mathrm{in}}$ being fixed by the airframe and $r_{\mathrm{tip}}$ taken as the design variable), and the chord $c$, which bounds the chordwise wetting. The submerged chord $c_{\mathrm{sub}} = \min(z/\sin{\beta},\,c)$ grows with the penetration depth in the shallow phase of the contact and saturates at the physical chord once the plate is fully wetted, at depths beyond $h^{*} = c\sin{\beta}$.
\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

The coupled vertical and rotational dynamics
of~\eqref{eq:vertical_dynamics_function}
and~\eqref{eq:rotational_dynamics_function} are integrated numerically through
a single water contact.  The purpose is narrow: to predict the vertical
velocity and the spin with which the vehicle leaves the water, given the
condition in which it arrives, so that the foil inclination can be chosen and
the vehicle checked to eject at all.  Starting from the instant the foils touch
the surface, the state $(z,\dot{z},\omega)$ is advanced with a fixed-step
semi-implicit Euler scheme until $z$ returns to the surface with an upward
velocity.  The outputs are the exit spin $\omega_{\mathrm{exit}}$ and the exit
vertical velocity $\dot{z}_{\mathrm{exit}}$.

\paragraph{Parameters.}
Table~\ref{tab:sim_params} lists the values used.  The vehicle parameters are
those of the as-built airframe of Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperone}.  The entry
spin $\omega_0 = 8000\;\mathrm{deg\,s^{-1}}$ is the rate measured in
unpowered descent, when the lift rotors are off and only the tangential rotors
drive the rotation.  The entry speeds bracket the measured range of $-1.53$ to
$-1.85\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ from Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperthree}.  The three
inclinations span what can be moulded on this airframe and include
$\beta = 30^\circ$, the only angle fabricated and flown.

The moment of inertia is the one parameter that has never been measured.  It is
taken as a thin hoop of the vehicle mass at the motor-mount circle,
$I = m r_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}$, on the grounds that the four motors sit on that
circle and the legs hang from them while the battery and flight board sit near
the centre.  This is a placeholder.  Since $\dot{\omega} = -Q/I$, the predicted
spin loss scales inversely with it, and the comparison below shows the
prediction to be sensitive to the choice.

\paragraph{Predicted contacts.}
Figure~\ref{fig:hop_time_history} overlays the nine contacts obtained by
crossing the three inclinations with the three entry speeds.  All nine eject.
The contact lasts $3.8$ to $8.0\;\mathrm{ms}$, the vehicle leaves climbing at
$0.77$ to $1.69\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and between $35\%$ and $95\%$ of the
entry spin remains.

The ordering follows from the element forces.  A faster entry raises the inflow
angle $\gamma$ and hence the angle of attack, so the vertical force and the
resisting torque both increase: the hop grows and more spin is spent.  A
steeper foil does the same through $\beta$ directly, and additionally reaches
full wetting sooner, since $h^{*} = c\sin\beta$.  The two effects compound, so
the heaviest loading occurs at the steep, fast corner: at $\beta = 45^\circ$
and $-3\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ only $35\%$ of the spin survives the contact,
against $95\%$ at $\beta = 10^\circ$ and $-1\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

The spin falls monotonically through every contact and never recovers, because
the resisting torque $Q$ is of one sign whenever the foil is wetted.  The
contact is therefore not a conservative bounce: it converts spin into vertical
momentum, and the spin must be restored by the tangential rotors during the
flight that follows.

\subsection{Comparison with the measured contacts}
\label{sec:model_validation}

Table~\ref{tab:model_vs_measured} places the model at the as-built inclination
and the measured entry speed beside the eight contacts reported in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperthree}.  The three predictions do not fail together,
and the pattern of agreement is informative.

\begin{table}[t]
    \centering
    \caption{The model at $\beta = 30^\circ$ and $\dot{z}_0 = -1.7\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
    against the eight measured contacts of Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperthree}.}
    \label{tab:model_vs_measured}
    \begin{tabular}{lrrl}
        \toprule
        & Predicted & Measured & \\
        \midrule
        Exit velocity          & $+1.20\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ & $+1.01\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ & agrees to $7\%$ \\
        Spin retained          & $75\%$                      & $\approx 40\%$              & high by $1.9\times$ \\
        Contact duration       & $4.6\;\mathrm{ms}$          & $118\;\mathrm{ms}$          & low by $26\times$ \\
        Mean vertical acceleration & $64\,g$                 & $2.4\,g$                    & high by $27\times$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The exit velocity, which is the quantity the design depends on, is predicted to
within $7\%$.  The total impulse is therefore close to right: the model gives a
velocity reversal of $2.90\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ against a measured
$2.71\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.  What the model distributes incorrectly is the
interval over which that impulse is delivered.  It predicts a brief, violent
contact of a few milliseconds at some $64\,g$; the measured contact delivers
almost the same momentum over more than a hundred milliseconds at a few $g$,
with the peak accelerations of
Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperthree} reaching only $4.9$ to $5.7\,g$.

The flat-surface assumption accounts for this.  The model wets the foil to its
geometric depth in an undisturbed surface, so the foil meets undisplaced water
at full density from the first instant and the force rises immediately to its
maximum.  A real surface yields: the water is pushed down and outward ahead of
the foil, the foil follows the depression it creates, and it remains engaged
far longer at a much lower load.  Integrated over the contact the two give
comparable impulses, which is why the exit velocity survives while the duration
does not.

A related symptom is visible in the predicted depths of
Figure~\ref{fig:hop_time_history}.  No case penetrates beyond about
$4.5\;\mathrm{mm}$, whereas full wetting of the as-built foil requires
$h^{*} = c\sin\beta = 7.5\;\mathrm{mm}$.  The chord cap in
$c_{\mathrm{sub}} = \min(z/\sin\beta,\,c)$ is therefore never reached at
$\beta = 30^{\circ}$, and the foil is ejected while still only partly wetted.
A contact that lasted as long as the measured one would drive the foil far
deeper, which is a further indication that the surface is not behaving as the
model assumes.

The over-predicted spin retention has the same origin, compounded by the
unmeasured inertia.  A contact that is too short removes too little spin,
and the hoop placeholder for $I$ is an upper bound on a rim-weighted vehicle
whose battery and board sit near the axis; both errors push the retained spin
upward.

\subsection{Energy accounting}
\label{sec:energy_accounting}

The contact converts rotational energy into height.  Its efficiency is defined
here as
%
\begin{equation}
    \eta = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2} m \dot{z}_{\mathrm{exit}}^{2}}
                {\tfrac{1}{2} I \left(\omega_0^{2} - \omega_{\mathrm{exit}}^{2}\right)},
    \label{eq:hop_efficiency}
\end{equation}
%
the vertical kinetic energy carried away from the surface divided by the
rotational energy given up to produce it.

One part of this ledger does not depend on the unmeasured inertia.  The
fraction of rotational energy consumed is
$1 - (\omega_{\mathrm{exit}}/\omega_0)^{2}$, a ratio in which $I$ cancels.
Across the nine simulated contacts it runs from $9\%$ at the shallowest and
gentlest entry to $88\%$ at the steepest and fastest.  At the as-built
$\beta = 30^{\circ}$ and the measured entry speed the model gives $44\%$, and
the measured spin retention of roughly $40\%$ implies $84\%$.  A single contact
therefore consumes most of the stored rotational energy, and the tangential
rotors must restore it before the next hop.

The efficiency itself does depend on $I$, and scales as $1/I$.  With the hoop
placeholder of Table~\ref{tab:sim_params} the model gives $\eta \approx 3\%$ at
the as-built condition, and the measured spin loss gives $\eta \approx 1\%$.
These figures should be read as an order of magnitude rather than as
measurements, but their smallness is not an artefact of the placeholder: the
rotational energy stored at $8000\;\mathrm{deg\,s^{-1}}$ exceeds the vertical
kinetic energy of a hop by more than an order of magnitude on any plausible
value of $I$.

The efficiency also separates the three inclinations in a way the exit velocity
alone does not.  At every entry speed $\beta = 45^{\circ}$ is dominated: it
produces both a smaller hop and a lower efficiency than $\beta = 30^{\circ}$,
because the steeper plate raises drag faster than lift and spends the extra
torque without returning extra impulse.  The comparison between $10^{\circ}$
and $30^{\circ}$ is a genuine trade rather than a domination.  At an entry of
$-2\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ the shallower foil delivers
$1.25\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ against $1.36\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, some $92\%$ of
the hop, for $\eta = 8.6\%$ against $3.4\%$, roughly two and a half times the
efficiency.  A shallow foil is therefore the better converter and a moderate
one the better jumper, and the choice depends on whether height per contact or
energy per contact is the binding constraint.  This thesis takes height, since
the vehicle must clear the surface reliably and the rotors restore the spin
between hops in any case.

These efficiencies inherit the error identified in
Section~\ref{sec:model_validation}: $\eta$ depends entirely on the predicted
spin loss, which the model under-states.  The ordering is nonetheless robust,
since it follows from the angle of attack alone, a shallower foil generating
less resisting torque per unit of vertical force.  The magnitudes should be
treated as indicative.

The consequence is worth stating plainly.  The hop is not an efficient energy
converter, and nothing in this thesis claims that hopping is cheaper than
hovering or than a conventional take-off from the surface.  What the contact
provides is a means of leaving the water at all without accelerating from rest
against surface adhesion, using a rotation the vehicle must sustain in any case
to remain controllable.  The spin is better understood as a buffer that the
contact draws on and the rotors refill than as a store from which useful work
is efficiently extracted.

\paragraph{What the model is for.}
On this evidence the model is a sizing tool and not a predictive one.  It
answers the question the design poses, whether a given foil at a given spin
will eject the vehicle and at roughly what speed.  It should not be used to
predict how long the foils stay wetted, how deep they go, or how much spin
survives.  Only one inclination was fabricated and flown, so the ordering the
model produces across $\beta$ is untested against experiment and is reported
as a prediction rather than a result.  The inclination
$\beta = 30^\circ$ was selected on the exit velocity it delivers at the
measured entry conditions, with the predicted spin loss used only as a
qualitative check that the vehicle would not be left uncontrollable on exit,
not as a quantitative criterion; the resulting geometry is described in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:paperone}.  Closing the gap on the contact
duration requires a free-surface treatment, which is identified as future work
in Chapter~\ref{chapter:conclusion}.
